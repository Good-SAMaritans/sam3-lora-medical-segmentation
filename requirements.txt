Good SAMaritans
Project Title
Segment Anything, Medically: Strategies for Adapting SAM to Medical Imaging
Group Members
Nikhil Sulay, Renzo Silvera, Razeen Basunia, Shin Koo Kang
Project Summary
Medical image segmentation, delineating organs and lesions at the pixel level, is central to
diagnosis and treatment planning, but training a separate model for each modality or task
is slow and labor intensive. Foundation models such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM)
could remove that per-task burden, but SAM is trained on natural images and performs
poorly on medical data, especially on targets with weak boundaries or low contrast. This
project investigates how to adapt SAM into an effective medical segmenter: why it fails out
of the box, how fine-tuning changes its behavior, and which adaptation strategy works
best. Prior work (MedSAM) adapted SAM using a single full fine-tuning recipe, so our
distinct contribution is a controlled comparison of strategies, decoder-only versus full finetuning, alongside an analysis of where adaptation helps and how an adapted foundation
model compares to a specialist trained from scratch. Since segmentation is a learned
dense-prediction task and SAM is built on a Vision Transformer, this is fundamentally a
problem of transfer, architecture, and optimization.
Approach
We will start from pretrained SAM and fine-tune it into a domain-adapted medical
segmenter, implementing the fine-tuning pipeline ourselves, including the bounding-box
prompt simulation that turns ground-truth masks into the box prompts SAM expects (with
random perturbations, following MedSAM). The central experiment is a controlled
comparison of adaptation strategies, decoder-only (freezing the encoder), LoRA (low-rank
updates injected into the encoder's attention layers), and full fine-tuning, holding data and
schedule fixed so differences are attributable to the strategy. We will benchmark these
against zero-shot SAM (lower baseline) and a U-Net we implement and train from scratch
(specialist comparison), letting us ask whether adapting a foundation model beats training
a small specialist directly. We will work in PyTorch and analyze the training process itself:
loss curves, overfitting, hyperparameter choices, and how each strategy trades trainable
parameters for accuracy.
Resources / Related Work & Papers
The state of the art for general segmentation is the Segment Anything Model (Kirillov et al.,
2023), a promptable foundation model trained on roughly a billion masks that segments
objects from point or box prompts. MedSAM (Ma et al., 2024) is our direct precedent: it
fine-tunes SAM on over a million medical image-mask pairs across ten modalities, matching
or beating modality-specific specialists, while documenting that out-of-the-box SAM fails on
weak-boundary targets. MedSAM used a single full fine-tuning recipe, so our work differs
by systematically comparing adaptation strategies and analyzing where adaptation helps,
rather than by maximizing scale. For the specialist baseline we draw on U-Net
(Ronneberger et al., 2015) and Attention U-Net (Oktay et al., 2018), the standard medicalsegmentation architectures. Our Dice and boundary-distance metrics follow common
practice and the MedSAM evaluation.
Datasets
We propose two datasets, both public with expert masks, so we need not annotate data.
• Kvasir-SEG (GI polyp segmentation, endoscopy, ~1,000 images, binary masks):
https://datasets.simula.no/kvasir-seg/ . A well-known benchmark with many
published baselines, letting us compare against reported numbers. MedSAM found
SAM already does relatively well on endoscopy polyps, so this is our "SAM starts
strong" case.
• MedSegBench (35 standardized medical segmentation datasets, pip-installable):
https://medsegbench.github.io/ . We will use one weak-boundary modality (for
example an ultrasound set) as the contrasting "SAM starts poorly" case, where
adaptation should help most.
This pairing gives an easy-versus-hard modality comparison with little data-wrangling. We
may add MedSegBench modalities later to broaden the analysis (see Scope). MedSegBench
is released under non-commercial Creative Commons licenses, which is fine for a course
project.
Evaluation Plan
We will measure quality with the Dice similarity coefficient (region overlap) and Normalized
Surface Distance (boundary agreement), matching MedSAM so we can compare where
applicable. Core comparisons are zero-shot SAM versus our adaptation strategies
(decoder-only, LoRA, full fine-tuning) versus a from-scratch U-Net, on each dataset, with
training and validation loss curves reported. The analysis we treat as central is where
adaptation helps: per-structure breakdowns, behavior on weak- versus distinct-boundary
targets, qualitative overlays comparing zero-shot and adapted outputs, and which strategy
recovers the most performance per trainable parameter. As a smaller supporting
experiment we will study how performance scales with the number of fine-tuning images.
Risks and Scope
The main risk is implementation complexity: SAM is promptable, so our training loop must
simulate box prompts from masks, which we expect to take a few days to get right.
Compute is manageable rather than a blocker: we will develop and run lighter experiments
on Colab Pro and use the PACE-ICE SLURM cluster for heavier fine-tuning and ablation
sweeps, with frequent checkpointing. The remaining risk is scope creep, so we separate
required from stretch work and will not expand the required set just because more
compute is available.
Required: zero-shot SAM baseline; decoder-only, LoRA, and full fine-tuning; a from-scratch
U-Net comparison; on two datasets (Kvasir-SEG and one weak-boundary MedSegBench
modality).
Stretch: adapters as an additional parameter-efficient variant; additional MedSegBench
modalities for a broader cross-modality study; the data-efficiency curve.
Ethical Implications
Medical segmentation models can influence diagnosis and treatment, so a high benchmark
Dice score must not be mistaken for clinical readiness; our work is a research study, not a
deployment tool. We will note dataset limitations, including modality imbalance and source
demographics, which can bias performance, and avoid overclaiming generalization beyond
the modalities and tasks we test.
